\begin{abstract}
Turning a natural-language request into calls against a fixed, typed API is a
smaller problem than the general-purpose models usually pointed at it. We treat
it as a translation layer rather than a language model: \thimble{} is a
48.12M-parameter model that reads a catalog of JSON function schemas and a
request, and emits the calls to make --- or an empty list --- through a
grammar-constrained decoder. The tokenizer, the training loss, and the decoder
are designed together around the same five decision points, rather than a
grammar being bolted onto a free-text model at inference time. Two measurements
suggest the co-design is load-bearing: constrained and unconstrained decoding
agree on 150 of 150 evaluation rows (there is no ``projection tax'' to
recover), and down-weighting the grammar-forced tokens in the loss --- on the
theory that the decoder supplies them anyway --- costs 12 points in a
controlled twin run, a small-scale counterexample to a null result reported at
larger scale. On catalogs represented in training, \thimble{} scores 86.3 on
Mobile Actions and 52.5 on DroidCall (ordered strict exact match) against a
published 45M-parameter yardstick's 63.7 and 17.0; on unseen catalogs it drops
to rough parity, and we show the failure is function selection, not argument
extraction. Alongside the model we publish eleven negative results, each killed
by a controlled measurement rather than an argument, and a single-file
dependency-free C inference engine whose fp32 output is byte-identical to the
reference stack on every row checked. Total training cost across all cycles was
about \$260. Weights, training pipeline, evaluation harness, and the full
chronological experimental record are released under MIT.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most systems that need tool calling obtain it from a general-purpose model
\citep{toolformer,gorilla}: either a frontier model prompted with function
schemas, or a sub-billion-parameter model fine-tuned for the task
\citep{tinyagent,octopusv2,xlam}. Both inherit machinery the task does not
obviously need --- open-vocabulary generation, world knowledge, conversational
ability --- and both keep a failure mode the task cannot tolerate: nothing
structurally prevents malformed JSON, invented parameter names, or calls to
undeclared functions. Constrained decoding
\citep{gcd2023,domino,xgrammar,gad} removes those failure modes at inference
time, but the standard arrangement bolts a grammar onto a model that was
trained for free text and then manages the mismatch: token healing at
constraint boundaries, logit masking over a vocabulary whose merges span
structural characters, and a documented distortion of the model's distribution
\citep{gad,dccd}.

This report explores the opposite end of the design space. If the model will
only ever emit tool calls under a grammar, the tokenizer, the training loss,
and the decoder can be designed together around the decisions the grammar
actually leaves open. In \thimble{} those decisions number five: refuse or
call; which tool; include this optional argument; what value; stop or
continue. Everything else --- braces, quotes, commas, and every argument key
--- is emitted by a grammar compiled from the user's schemas, so three
properties hold on any catalog with no training or configuration: output is
always well-formed JSON; argument keys can only come from the schema, making
parameter-name hallucination structurally impossible rather than unlikely; and
calls to undeclared tools cannot be emitted. The whole model is 48.12M
parameters, small enough that specializing a private copy to one API surface
is routine.

We are explicit about what is and is not claimed. Small tool-calling-only
models exist: \needle{} \citep{needle2,san} occupies the same parameter class
with far larger training resources, and our headline tables use their
published numbers, on their metric, as a yardstick --- not as a controlled
comparison (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). A token-level loss-weighting scheme very
similar to ours appears in the \needle{} authors' architecture notes
\citep{san}; we do not claim it as a contribution. Training-time exploitation
of output grammars has precedent \citep{graft,slgm,ailem2021}. The
contributions here are narrower:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A case study in tokenizer--loss--decoder co-design} for
  grammar-constrained generation, with the two measurements that make the case:
  the absence of a projection tax (\S\ref{sec:notax}), and a 12-point loss from
  down-weighting grammar-forced tokens (\S\ref{sec:rft}) --- evidence that
  forced structural tokens carry call-sequencing signal even though the model
  is never asked to produce them. The latter is a small-scale counterexample to
  the null result of \citet{gan2025}, who found loss-masking of constrained
  spans made no significant difference when fine-tuning 1B--8B models.
  \item \textbf{Eleven negative results} (\S\ref{sec:negative}), each with the
  controlled measurement that killed it, including two process failures that
  cost real points. Several reverse conclusions that would have shipped on
  intuition and on literature support.
  \item \textbf{An open, end-to-end artifact}: weights, the full training and
  synthesis pipeline, the evaluation harness, an adaptation script targeting
  new catalogs, the complete chronological lab record, and a single-file
  dependency-free C inference engine byte-identical to the reference
  implementation on every row checked (\S\ref{sec:engine}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Constrained decoding.} Grammar-constrained decoding for structured
NLP is well developed \citep{gcd2023,domino,xgrammar}, including analyses of
the distortion constraints induce on a free-text model's distribution
\citep{gad} and draft-then-constrain schemes that recover accuracy lost to
that distortion \citep{dccd}. All of this work treats the model as fixed.
\thimble{} differs in premise: the model is trained from scratch for exactly
the constrained task, and \S\ref{sec:notax} measures the resulting distortion
at approximately zero, which is why draft-then-constrain had nothing to
recover here.

\paragraph{Structure-aware training.} GraFT \citep{graft} applies grammar
masking during training for natural-language-to-temporal-logic translation and
argues theoretically that masking invalid tokens accelerates convergence. SLGM
\citep{slgm} combines format-aware losses with format-constrained decoding for
structured prediction with $<$1B models. \citet{ailem2021} bias an NMT loss
toward terminology-constraint tokens. Closest to our loss question,
\citet{gan2025} explicitly tested masking the loss on grammar-constrained
spans when fine-tuning LLMs for coreference and found no significant effect.
Our controlled twin run finds the opposite at 48M scale trained from scratch
(\S\ref{sec:rft}); we conjecture the difference is that a from-scratch
specialist must learn sequencing structure that a pretrained LLM already has.

\paragraph{Small tool-calling models.} TinyAgent \citep{tinyagent} fine-tunes
1.1B/7B models for edge function calling; Octopus~v2 \citep{octopusv2} trains
a 2B on-device model with functional tokens; xLAM \citep{xlam} releases a
family of action models down to 1B. Empirical studies of small models on
function calling consistently find them capable but brittle off-distribution
\citep{smallbig}, consistent with our own in-domain/out-of-domain split. In
the sub-100M class, \needle{} \citep{needle2,san} is the published reference
point: a 45M attention-only architecture trained on 153B tokens with
quantization-aware training and a production C++ engine. \thimble{} reaches its parameter class from a different
direction --- a boring dense trunk, $\sim$1B unique training tokens, no
pretraining phase, and the co-design thesis above --- which makes the
comparison informative even where it is not controlled.

\paragraph{Retrieval and selection.} The decoder refreshes its tool-candidate
set between emitted calls, conditioning on the partial plan, following the
dynamic tool retrieval argument of \citet{dtdr}. Tool-name selection is
treated as a readout over candidate name spans in the prompt rather than
open-vocabulary generation, following the diagnosis of \citet{lnp} that
mis-selection is a readout failure; \S\ref{sec:results} confirms their
prediction that the readout helps only off-distribution.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The five decisions}

A canonical call array over a catalog leaves the model exactly five kinds of
choice: (1) refuse or call, after the opening bracket; (2) which tool, among
retrieved candidates; (3) include this optional argument, for each optional
key in canonical order; (4) what value, for each included argument; (5) stop
or continue, after each call. The grammar, compiled from the catalog's JSON
schemas, emits everything else. Argument keys are iterated from the schema, so
there is no step at which an invented key could be produced.

\subsection{Tokenizer}

The tokenizer is built for the grammar rather than for compression. JSON
structural characters (\verb|{ } [ ] , : "|) are singleton tokens excluded
from merging, so no token ever spans a value/structure boundary: structure can
be force-fed exactly, with no token healing and no ambiguity about where a
constraint lands. Digits are also singletons and never merge --- BPE over our
corpus had produced \texttt{2021} as one token but \texttt{38.0} as three, and
copy errors like $300\to30000$ traced directly to inconsistent numeric
segmentation. The 16{,}384-entry vocabulary was verified by a fragmentation
gate (word-value fragmentation 2.72$\to$2.34 tokens/word, digit values
lengthening by design). The rebuild shipped inside a bundle (with 350k added
corpus rows and mix reweighting) that moved tool-name-sequence accuracy from
80.4\% to 91.5\%; the tokenizer's isolated share was not measured, which we
note rather than obscure.

\subsection{Loss}

Cross-entropy is weighted by decision type: structure 1$\times$, argument keys
1.5$\times$, tool names 2$\times$, argument values 4$\times$, and the
stop/continue token 6$\times$, matched to the measured error distribution. A
closely related scheme (structure 1$\times$, keys 1.5$\times$, names
2$\times$, values 4$\times$) appears independently in the \needle{}
architecture notes \citep{san}; the stop-decision term is ours, motivated by
the multi-call collapse diagnosed in \S\ref{sec:training}. The important
finding about this loss is negative-space: the grammar-forced tokens, 46.4\%
of the weighted loss budget, must \emph{not} be down-weighted
(\S\ref{sec:rft}).

\subsection{Decoder}

The decoder consults the model only at the five decision points.
Structural branches are decided by first-token logits, which is exact because
options begin with distinct singleton tokens. Literal choices (tool names,
enum values, optional-inclusion) are scored by length-normalized
teacher-forced log-probability with exact snapshot/rollback of the KV cache.
String values are generated freely until the closing-quote singleton, with
special tokens banned and periodic $k$-gram repetition ($k\le4$, three
consecutive periods) treated as a stop; an earlier blanket repeated-trigram
ban mangled long natural values and cost 51 points on Mobile Actions before
being diagnosed. Number values are generated under a numeric-character
constraint with a structural-preference stop. Between calls the candidate set
is re-retrieved conditioned on the partial plan \citep{dtdr}. Tool-name choice
ensembles a trained bilinear readout over candidate name spans \citep{lnp}
with the LM's teacher-forced score; a lexical prior over the catalog is
blended in only in proportion to how sharply it discriminates \emph{and} how
uncertain the model is, which matters for refusal calibration on single-tool
catalogs.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:training}

\paragraph{Data.} The corpus mixes public tool-calling sets (xLAM, ToolACE,
Seal-Tools and DroidCall train splits, dria-pythonic, hermes-fc, Mobile
Actions train) with teacher-synthesized rows validated against parameter
types and an evidence rule: every scalar argument value must be evidenced in
the query, because wrong argument values were the largest error source and
rows that reward inventing values are worse than no rows. Contamination
against all evaluation queries was verified by exact normalized overlap and an
8-gram firewall (one coincidental collision found and removed); the firewall
is also applied to our own synthesized data.

\paragraph{Error-driven iteration.} Row accuracy factors as
$P(\text{name sequence})\times p^{n}$ for per-call argument accuracy $p$. Each
version measured which factor was binding and attacked only that. The v6
round is representative: of 193 failing calls on the previous checkpoint, 66
added exactly one unmentioned optional argument, $\sim$35 bound the wrong
entity, $\sim$30 missed canonical date forms, and $\sim$29 were unwinnable
gold-typing noise (\S\ref{sec:negative}). Three matching synthesis modes
(74{,}250 accepted rows, \$56) were annealed into the learning-rate decay
phase of a continued run (no re-warmup, following \citealp{parmar2024}) rather
than retrained from scratch: the same corrective corpus scored 28.4 fed from
step zero and \textbf{33.1} annealed into decay, with a mid-run probe at
constant learning rate attributing $+3.3$ points to the corpus itself.
Corrective data dilutes when it competes with the whole corpus and
concentrates when it arrives late; timing is a hyperparameter of the dataset.
This replicates, at 48M scale and under a controlled from-scratch twin, the
decay-phase data curricula of MiniCPM \citep{minicpm} and OLMo~2
\citep{olmo2}, and the continued-pretraining recipe of \citet{parmar2024},
which independently locates the distribution switch late in the decay
schedule; the controlled twin --- same corrective corpus, scratch versus
anneal --- is the part those reports do not isolate.

\paragraph{Trunk.} The architecture is deliberately standard: $d{=}448$, 20
layers, GQA 8/4, SwiGLU $2.0\times$, QK-norm, sandwich RMSNorm, tied
embeddings, 768-token context, Muon \citep{muon} on 2D weights with AdamW
elsewhere. A
controlled study by the \needle{} authors \citep{san} finds architecture
choices at this scale worth hundredths of a nat at matched parameters; our
experience matched --- every real gain came from tokenizer, loss, or data
changes --- so the trunk is not where any claim lives.

category traces to selection over unfamiliar schema dialects

Draft-then-constrain methods \citep{dccd} recover accuracy that constrained
decoding costs a free-text model; the distortion they correct is real and
measured in that setting \citep{gad}. On \thimble{} the tax measures $-1.3$
and $0.0$ (Table~\ref{tab:notax}): the model already wants what the grammar
enforces, because it was never trained to want anything else. Stated plainly:
the grammar buys \emph{reliability}, not accuracy --- malformed output and
invented parameter names become unreachable, and the $\sim$11\% of Seal rows
where free generation emits invalid JSON remain parseable --- but
``constrained decoding makes the model correct'' is a different claim and not
one this data supports.

\subsection{Grammar-forced tokens carry call-sequencing signal}
\label{sec:rft}

If the decoder force-feeds all structure, training the model to predict those
tokens looks like waste --- they are 46.4\% of the weighted loss budget. In a
controlled twin run (identical data, architecture, and budget; forced-token
weight 0.1$\times$), the down-weighted twin finished about \textbf{12 points
behind everywhere}, trailing by 19 points on name-sequencing at matched
mid-training steps. Structure tokens are where the model learns when a call
ends and whether another follows; remove the pressure to predict them and
multi-call sequencing degrades. \citet{gan2025} report that the analogous
intervention (loss-masking constrained spans) made no significant difference
when fine-tuning 1B--8B pretrained models --- consistent with our conjecture
that pretrained models already possess the sequencing structure a from-scratch
specialist must learn from exactly these tokens.

\subsection{The name head helps only where the model is uncertain}

Ablating the span-readout name head on the v3 checkpoint: exactly zero effect
on Mobile Actions (trunk name accuracy already 99.3\%), $+2.6$ exact /
$+20.5$ name points on Seal in-domain, and $+4.0$ exact / $+23.4$ name points
($+39\%$ relative) out-of-domain --- precisely the pattern \citet{lnp}
predicts for a readout fix. The evaluation-practice lesson was sharper than
the result: an in-distribution ablation alone would have deleted the
component worth the most on the suite the model was failing.

\section{Eleven negative results}
\label{sec:negative}

Each idea below was killed by a controlled measurement. Several had
literature support; two reversed conclusions that would have shipped on
intuition. Full details and the chronological record are in the repository's
\texttt{FINDINGS.md} and \texttt{RESULTS.md}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Word-span copying of argument values: $-30$ points} (40.0
$\to$ 10.0 on Seal single-call, names unchanged). The supporting evidence was
real --- 90\% of gold values appear verbatim in the query; Seal-Tools' own
error analysis attributes 70\% of parameter errors to extraction; stale and
incorrect value propagation is a documented failure class \citep{funcbenchgen}
--- but
``verbatim'' had been measured as \emph{substring} containment while the
mechanism enumerated \emph{word-level} spans. Gold \texttt{manta ray} is a
substring of ``manta rays'' but equals no word span of it. A premise can be
right while its operationalization is wrong.
\item \textbf{A learned pointer/copy head: $-16$ points} (25.3 $\to$ 9.3,
names unchanged). The head learns --- roughly half of training samples hit
both endpoints exactly --- but its confidence does not correlate with its
correctness, so any confidence gate admits wrong spans, and a wrong span is a
guaranteed row failure where free generation often produced the right value.
Gating requires calibration, not accuracy.
\item \textbf{Down-weighting grammar-forced tokens: $-12$ points}
(\S\ref{sec:rft}).
\item \textbf{From-scratch retraining vs.\ annealing: $-4.7$ points} (28.4 vs
33.1, same corrective corpus; \S\ref{sec:training}).
\item \textbf{Field-set reranking: $-1.4$ points} (fixed 3 rows, broke 13).
It targeted the key-set error bucket, which training had already fixed by the
time it ran. Re-diagnose before implementing; fixes have a shelf life.
\item \textbf{Beam search / RL / best-of-$N$: oracle-capped below target.}
pass@$k$ \citep{codex} with $k{=}9$ --- perfect selection over nine samples,
which no deployable reranker achieves --- plateaued at exactly $+5.0$ at
every sample size measured,
reaching 29.3 where 32.6 was needed. For most failing rows the correct call
array is not in the model's distribution at any temperature; the gap is
capability, not search.
\item \textbf{Draft-then-constrain: nothing to recover} (\S\ref{sec:notax}).
Measure the quantity a technique recovers before implementing the technique.
\item \textbf{A global optional-inclusion prior: catalog-dependent, rejected
before shipping.} Gold optional-inclusion rates range from 94.1\% (Mobile
Actions) to 33.4\% (Seal-Tools) across sources; a constant that helps one
catalog wrecks another. Inclusion rate is a property of the catalog, which
makes it a training problem, not a decoding constant.
\item \textbf{Raising \texttt{MAX\_CALLS} 4 $\to$ 6: $0.000$ change.} The cap
was never binding; long rows fail for other reasons.
\item \textbf{RLOO \citep{rloo} on the annealed checkpoint: diverges at every
learning rate tried} (reward $0.50\to-0.12$ by update 200 at $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$;
healthy to update 550 then $0.92\to0.13$ at $5\mathrm{e}{-6}$), while the same
recipe ran stably on the conventionally-decayed sibling. Decay-phase annealing
appears to leave the model in a sharper minimum that policy-gradient noise
unravels; anneal and RL are in tension.
\item \textbf{Matching the benchmark's numeric typing: not learnable.}
Seal-Tools' gold stores numerically-typed arguments as strings 13.2\% of the
time with 74\% of parameters inconsistent; a perfect per-parameter policy
ceilings at 87.8\% against 86.4\% for one global rule. Some of the gap between
a model and a benchmark is the benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

Two process failures cost real points and are reported with the same
prominence. \emph{The pre-registered champion selector picked the wrong
model}: held-out loss on the general mix chose a scratch twin over the
annealed one by five-thousandths of a nat, and the scratch twin scored 28.4
against 33.1 --- a general-mix dev set is structurally biased against a model
that just annealed \emph{away} from that mix, so selection must use an
eval-distribution-flavored (but eval-free) split whenever recipes diverge in
training distribution. \emph{A truncating default silently dropped data}: a
packing default of 512 tokens dropped 47{,}551 rows --- precisely the longest,
multi-call rows the model was weakest on --- while reporting success.

\section{Reproducibility and the C engine}
\label{sec:engine}

The repository contains the full pipeline --- download, conversion,
contamination firewall, packing, training, and evaluation --- with the exact
commands that rebuild the published numbers, plus \texttt{scripts/adapt.py},
which runs the same synthesize/pack/anneal recipe against a user-supplied
catalog (the v6 cycle's synthesis cost \$56 for 74{,}250 validated rows). No
third-party catalog has yet been adapted end-to-end and published; the recipe
is measured, the ergonomics are new.

Inference is additionally implemented as a single dependency-free C file
(\texttt{cengine/thimble.c}: tokenizer, trunk, grammar decoder, name head,
and retrieval, $\sim$1{,}600 lines, C11 + libm). Byte-exact parity with the
Python stack is the acceptance test: identical \texttt{dumps\_calls} output on
100/100 Mobile Actions evaluation rows and all demo queries including the
refusal. With int8 weights (per-row scales, activations quantized per matvec
through NEON \texttt{sdot}), the engine is a 48MB artifact that loads in
$\sim$20ms and runs a full request --- prefill plus every decision, rollout,
and rollback --- in 348ms on an Apple M3, against 582ms for the PyTorch stack;
quantization changed 2 of 100 rows, both, coincidentally, toward gold.
Comparable engine techniques (skipping the vocabulary projection on forced
steps, int8 dot-product kernels, token-level validation against a reference)
are described for \needle{}'s production engine \citep{needle2}; ours differ
in being a byte-parity port of an open reference implementation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Single-seed results.} Every number here is one training run; the
project's own selector failure (28.4 vs 33.1 between sibling checkpoints)
is an implicit variance estimate large enough to swallow the Seal-in margin.
The two headline ablations (\S\ref{sec:notax}, \S\ref{sec:rft}) are
controlled twin runs but not multi-seed. We flag which claims rest on large
margins (Mobile Actions $+22.6$, DroidCall $+35.5$, the $-12$ and $-30$
ablations) and which are within noise (Seal-in $+0.5$).
\textbf{The yardstick is not a controlled baseline.} \needle{}'s numbers are
their published figures on their metric through their production engine;
precision asymmetry favors us, scope asymmetry favors us (they carry no
task-specific eval-catalog training data; our Mobile Actions and DroidCall
train splits are in the mix, disclosed per-suite), and training-resource
asymmetry favors them ($\sim$153B vs $\sim$1B tokens).
\textbf{Generalization.} Unseen catalogs drop to parity, BFCL to 23.5 vs
42.6; parallel instantiations of one schema are a distinct, worse failure
(12.0; live-parallel 0.0). The model's intended use is per-catalog
specialization, not open-world calling.
\textbf{Scope.} A 768-token context excludes 4.1\% of BFCL rows; Java and
JavaScript schema dialects are essentially absent from the corpus.
\textbf{Provenance.} The project was built by one person in a few days with
substantial AI assistance, for about \$260 of compute and API spend; this
report was likewise drafted with AI assistance and verified by the author.

\section{Conclusion}

Treated as a translation layer rather than a language model, tool calling
against a declared API fits in 48M parameters, and co-designing the tokenizer,
loss, and decoder around the grammar --- rather than constraining a free-text
model after the fact --- is measurably load-bearing: the constraint costs
nothing at decode time precisely because the model was trained inside it, and
the tokens the grammar supplies are, counterintuitively, tokens the model must
still learn. The negative results are offered as the most reusable part: most
of what this project learned, it learned by being wrong efficiently.
